“Should cats take half a capsule and monitor?”

Answered

Monitor, yes. Half a capsule, no. The caution is right; the mechanism is the wrong one, and it is the specific thing worth not doing.

Reason 1

It is the exact behaviour behind your only cat incident. An owner split one capsule between two cats and both vomited. Splitting means opening — those cats met loose, bitter, mucosally irritant powder in the mouth, which an intact capsule carries straight past. Putting that instruction on your website is publishing the one thing you already have a bad outcome from.

Reason 2

Half a capsule is not a measurable amount. An owner tipping out roughly half the powder lands anywhere within ±50%, and the error runs upward more often than down. You would be replacing a known dose with an unknown one in the species with the least margin.

Reason 3

It would contradict your label. The label will say up to 39 lb, one capsule. A website saying cats take half creates a mismatch — and when the two disagree, you have no good answer to which one the customer should have followed.

The trade

Half a capsule genuinely does halve the eugenol — 1.24 mg/kg rather than 2.48 for a 10 lb cat. But both sit over a hundred times below the dose that caused liver injury in a sensitised model, and buying that reduction costs you the irritation route, a measurable dose, and label consistency. Not worth it.

Put this on the site insteadCats: give one whole capsule daily with food. Do not open or split capsules — the powder is bitter and can upset the mouth and stomach. If your cat is under 8 lb, check with your veterinarian before starting. Start with a single capsule, then wait two days and see how your cat does before continuing. If you are ever unsure, give less rather than more.

What you were reaching for is the monitoring, and that part is exactly right. Keep “watch closely,” keep the sign list, keep permission to stop. Those are what turned both of your existing cases into non-events. The half-capsule idea was standing in for caution — the monitoring block does the same job without opening a capsule.

Rewriting the pumpkin seed section

Copy options

Pumpkin seed is the easiest of your four to write about, because it is the only one where you can lean on nutrition rather than herbal tradition. That matters: nutritive-value statements have historically been the one category FDA-CVM treated as acceptable for animal products — the classic examples being “calcium for proper bone structure” and “taurine for healthy heart function in cats.” Composition is factual, and food is food.

Three angles, none of which say anything about a parasite:

Angle

It is the mild one. Wormwood, black walnut and clove are concentrated, potent plants. Pumpkin seed is not. Saying so is honest, differentiating, and quietly reassures the customer who arrived worried about wormwood.

Angle

It is food. Plain pumpkin is already a familiar thing to put in a dog or cat’s bowl. That is a fact your reader can check against their own experience, which makes it more persuasive than anything you could assert.

Angle

The botany is genuinely interesting. Cucurbitin is an unusual non-protein amino acid that occurs in the seed and not the flesh, and it is named after the plant’s genus, Cucurbita. Stating that it is present is composition. Stating what it does is a claim. Say the first, stop there, and let a curious reader go looking.

Short — a product-page bulletPumpkin seed — the gentlest thing in the blend, and the only one that is also food.
Medium — a product-page paragraphPumpkin seed is the mild one. Wormwood, black walnut and clove are concentrated, potent plants; pumpkin seed is something dogs and cats already eat. It is naturally rich in zinc, magnesium, fibre and healthy fats, and it contains cucurbitin — an unusual amino acid found in the seed rather than the flesh, named for the plant's own genus, Cucurbita. Our formulator included it to keep the blend balanced rather than simply strong.
Long — for the blogOf the four plants in this blend, pumpkin seed is the one you have most likely already fed your pet. Plain pumpkin is a familiar thing to stir into a bowl, and the seed carries most of what makes it interesting: zinc, magnesium, fibre and healthy fats. It also contains cucurbitin, an unusual amino acid that occurs in the seed and not the flesh — unusual enough that it was named after the plant's own genus, Cucurbita. Pumpkin seeds appear in traditional herbal preparations across a lot of cultures and a long stretch of the written record, which is how they came to be paired with the other three in the first place. We include it because a blend of three potent herbs benefits from something gentle in it. It is the mildest ingredient in the capsule, and that is the job it is doing.

Two things to avoid in this section

Avoid

“Has been studied for…” Referencing research implies your product delivers what the research found — that is an implied claim, and it needs the same substantiation as saying it outright. You do not have to do the implying. State that the compound is present and stop.

Avoid

A heading that claims on the paragraph’s behalf. Clean copy under a heading like “How It Works” is still making the claim — the heading does it. Check what sits above these paragraphs; “What’s In It” or “The Four Plants” carries no assertion.

And fix the chemistry while you are here. Cucurbitin is the amino acid — 3-amino-pyrrolidine-3-carboxylic acid, found in the seed. Cucurbitacin B is a different molecule entirely, a triterpenoid. The current copy calls cucurbitacin an amino acid, which merges the two. Three letters apart, unrelated chemistry — and exactly the detail a sceptical vet or a competitor would use to argue the whole formulation rationale is unserious.

Notes

What not to change

  • Your brand voice. None of this requires you to sound clinical. Warm and plain-spoken is fine — it is the claims that need to go, not the personality.
  • The herb education. Traditional-use content is your best asset and it is safe when framed as history rather than performance. Keep writing it.
  • The product name, for now. “Dewormer” must go. “Parasite Cleanse” is a different order of risk and renaming costs you ranking, brand equity and every printed thing you own. Put it to an FDA/CVM attorney as its own question, later.
  • The formula. Decided — clove stays at 75 mg this cycle, behind the gates.

What to add that you do not have yet

Once the assays are back, the strongest page on your site will be a quality one. It carries no claim risk and almost nobody in this category can write it:

  • We publish every amount on the label. Nothing proprietary.
  • We assay our wormwood for thujone and hold each batch to a range.
  • We test for heavy metals, microbiology and pesticides — and mycotoxins on the black walnut.
  • We keep a certificate of analysis for every lot.
  • We set a maximum daily amount, a defined cycle and a rest period.
  • We tell you which animals should not take it, and exactly what to watch for.

Order of work

  • Today: the SEO title field, the mechanism sentence, and the Detox Bundle check. Three edits, maybe twenty minutes, and they remove most of the exposure.
  • This week: the gates and binder note on all three pages, the order-confirmation line, the customer email.
  • Next: the term sweep across everything, and the review-widget audit.
  • With the reprint: make the label and the site say the same thing. Mismatches between them are their own problem.

One last thing. Warnings are a weak control — people read them least when they are confident and in a hurry. What actually protects an animal is instructions with nothing to estimate, one default for every ambiguity, explicit permission to stop, signs in words an owner recognises, and a real person to reach. Most of the blocks above are built that way rather than as disclaimers, which is why they are worth more than their length suggests.
