Parasite Cleanse Dose Model

Working the exposure arithmetic behind 1 capsule per 20 lb — where the margin sits, where it thins, and the label language that keeps it there.

Basis 1 cap / 20 lb / day Actives Wormwood · Black walnut · Clove · Pumpkin seed Status Decisions locked, awaiting mg/capsule Revised 27 Aug 2026

Where this stands

Two changes are decided: a hard daily cap with a slow start, and label language that makes the 14-day stop unmistakable. Part I is the copy to hand your printer.

The rest phase is the better half of that second decision. A stop with nothing on the other side of it gets ignored; a stop that hands the owner something to do instead gets followed. It also happens to be the honest version of the protocol — the fourteen days off are doing real work, not just marking time.

The open item is unchanged and it is the one that governs everything downstream: wormwood’s thujone content varies across roughly a 500-fold range by chemotype and origin, so herb weight does not specify a dose. Until you assay it, the ceilings in Part II are theoretical.

Part I — Decisions locked

Both of your calls, written as label copy. Bracketed values are yours to set; everything else is ready to use.

Why each line is there

Your call — good one

The rest phase is what makes the stop stick

“Stop at day 14” on its own is a negative instruction, and negative instructions get skipped. Pairing it with something to do instead converts the pause into a phase — and your entire safety case for wormwood rests on that duration cap actually being observed, so this is a compliance mechanism as much as a merchandising one.

One caution on how you word the rest phase. Naming organs — “supports liver and gut health” — is a structure/function claim, which for animal products is a drug claim rather than a protected supplement claim. Describe the routine instead of the organ: “settle back into normal meals,” “a gentle two weeks,” “for the stretch between cycles.” You get the same customer behaviour without the exposure. Sell the rest phase hard; just sell it as a phase, not as an organ treatment.

Added

The seizure contraindication is not boilerplate

Worth knowing why it earns its line: thujone acts as a GABAA antagonist, which is mechanistically the same direction as lowering the seizure threshold. In the rodent studies, seizures were the defining toxic endpoint. An animal with an existing seizure history is the one population where the mechanism and the risk line up exactly.

Liver and kidney disease are on the list for the clove side — impaired clearance is what turns a comfortable eugenol margin into a thin one.

Added

The monitoring list is organised by mechanism, not by generic caution

Each line maps to a specific pathway, which is why they are worth keeping rather than trimming to “if any adverse reaction occurs”:

  • Tremors, wobbliness, disorientation — thujone, neurological.
  • Yellow gums or eyes, dark urine — eugenol, hepatic.
  • Pale, grey or muddy gums, fast breathing — methaemoglobinaemia, and the reason cats get their own mention. This is the sign an owner is least likely to recognise unprompted, and it is the one where hours matter.
  • GI upset, appetite, energy — the general early-warning band, and what the three-day half-dose is there to surface.
You decide

Two numbers still bracketed

Cycles per year. No regulator sets this. Four — roughly quarterly — is defensible, keeps total annual exposure to 56 days, and fits the seasonal rhythm owners already think in. Take it or set your own, but put a number on the label rather than leaving it open.

Minimum age. Needs your formulator or a vet. Young animals have immature hepatic clearance, which is the same argument as the liver-disease exclusion. Twelve weeks is a common industry floor; I would not pick it for you.

Part II — The model behind the cap

Everything runs off one conversion. At 1 capsule per 20 lb, and 20 lb being 9.07 kg, per-kilogram exposure is per-capsule mass divided by 9.07.

Exposure at label dose mg/kg/day  =  (mg per capsule) ÷ 9.07

Inverted — the ceiling you can formulate to max mg per capsule  =  (target mg/kg/day) × 9.07

Under linear per-weight dosing the mg/kg figure is constant across body weights, so one per-capsule ceiling governs the whole range. Two ceilings to set.

Thujone, from wormwood

The EMA accepts 3.0 mg thujone/day for a human for up to two weeks — across 70 kg that is 0.043 mg/kg/day. Against the rodent benchmark dose lower limit of 11 mg/kg/day for clonic seizures, that carries a margin of roughly 250×. In the absence of canine data it is the most defensible target available.

0.043 mg/kg/day × 9.07 kg  =  ≈ 0.39 mg thujone per capsule
Formulate to 0.35 mg and you hold working margin.

Eugenol, from clove

The WHO acceptable daily intake for eugenol is 2.5 mg/kg bw/day, giving ≈ 22.7 mg eugenol per capsule for a dog — around 150–225 mg of clove powder at 10–15% eugenol. For dogs, clove is comfortable. Cats are a separate calculation; see Part IV.

Why the cap matters at the top of the range

Metabolic clearance scales to roughly body weight0.75, not body weight1.0. Linear per-pound dosing tracks metabolism correctly at your 20 lb reference and drifts high above it, by (BW ÷ 9.07)0.25.

A 4-capsule ceiling binds from roughly 80 lb up, which is exactly where linear scaling starts running ahead of metabolism. It lands slightly below the allometric figure for a 150 lb dog — the conservative side, and the right side to err on. Small dogs are unaffected: linear dosing under-shoots below 20 lb, which is already the safe direction.

Part III — Why herb weight is not a dose

The open item, and the one worth taking to your formulator this week.

Thujone in dried wormwood is the product of two variables, each of which swings widely. Essential oil yield ranges from 0.2% to 1.5% of dried herb. Thujone as a fraction of that oil ranges from near-nothing to 65% — β-thujone alone has been measured from 0.1% to 64.6% across samples. At least six chemotypes of European A. absinthium are recognised, and several are not thujone chemotypes at all; they are dominated by sabinyl acetate or epoxyocimene and are effectively thujone-free.

Multiply the ranges and dried wormwood spans roughly 0.002% to 1.0% thujone — a 500-fold spread.

Specification gap

Same label, same weight, 100× difference in the active

Both failure directions are real and they fail differently. A low-thujone chemotype gives you a product that is safe and inert — customers see nothing, and your field results stop being reproducible. A high-thujone chemotype puts you several-fold over the only regulatory reference that supports the ingredient at all.

Neither is visible on a certificate that says “Artemisia absinthium, aerial parts, 175 mg.” Batch-to-batch drift in a botanical is the default, not the exception.

Action Add a thujone assay (GC-MS) to your incoming raw-material spec with a defined range — not just a maximum. Put it on the COA per batch. Then dose to assayed thujone and let herb weight float to hit it.

It is also your best answer to a challenge: “we assay every batch and hold to the EMA reference” is a different conversation from “our formulator has used it for years.”

This may also explain your field data. If results varied across years of testing, uncontrolled chemotype variation is a likelier explanation than anything about the animals. Specifying thujone makes the formula reproducible as well as defensible — worth more to you than the compliance argument.

What “dose to assayed thujone” actually means

Plainly: stop specifying the plant and start specifying the compound.

Your raw-material spec almost certainly reads something like the first line below. It names a plant and a weight, and because thujone varies 500-fold, it does not control the active at all. The second line does.

What you likely have now Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium), aerial parts, powdered — 175 mg

What controls the dose Wormwood herb powder, 0.15–0.25% total thujone (α + β) by GC-MS
Blended to deliver 0.35 mg thujone per capsule

The consequence is that herb weight becomes an output, not an input. A lot assaying at 0.20% thujone goes in at 175 mg. A lot assaying at 0.40% goes in at 87 mg. Both capsules deliver 0.35 mg of thujone, which is the thing your customer’s dog actually receives. This is ordinary practice for standardised botanicals — it is how anyone selling milk thistle specifies silymarin, or turmeric specifies curcuminoids. Wormwood is simply less often sold that way, which is why nobody has offered it to you.

“GC-MS” is just the analytical method — gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, the standard technique for volatile compounds like thujone. You name it in the spec so results are comparable between labs and lots. You are not buying an instrument; you are asking for a test.

Resolves a worry

A specification is not a claim — so there is nothing to prove to anyone

This distinction is worth being precise about, because the instinct behind the question is exactly right and the answer is better than you think.

  • A specification is a private purchasing document between you and your supplier. It says what you will accept. You are not asserting anything to the public, and no regulator asks you to prove it — the COA is the proof, and a lot that misses the range gets rejected rather than used. A spec you test against is not exposure; it is the control that removes exposure.
  • A label claim — “standardised to 0.2% thujone” printed on the package — is a public statement, and that one you must be able to substantiate for every lot you sell. A lot out of range there is misbranding.

So: put the range in the spec, keep it off the label. You get the entire benefit — controlled dosing, per-lot documentation, a defensible answer if challenged — and take on no public claim at all. Nothing obliges you to print thujone content on the package, and you should not, at least until you have enough lot history to know the range holds.

The general rule Internal documents record what you verify. Labels state what you can prove for every unit sold. Your caution was correct — it just belongs at the label, not at the spec.

You are further along than this section assumes. If your formulator already runs heavy metals, microbiological and pesticide testing, the lab relationship, the sampling discipline and the COA habit all exist. Adding one marker assay to an established panel is an incremental request, not a new quality programme — and it is a normal thing to ask a lab already handling your material. This is a smaller lift for you than for most brands at your size.

How to actually get it done

Step 1

Ask your current supplier for a COA showing thujone content by GC-MS on the lot you are using.

Free, and the answer is informative either way. Most bulk botanical suppliers test identity, heavy metals, microbiology and pesticides — but not marker compounds on non-standardised herbs. If they cannot produce it, you now know your active has never been measured.

Step 2

If they cannot, send a retain sample to a third-party lab that does botanical marker assays for the supplement industry.

Eurofins and Alkemist Labs are both well established in this space; your manufacturer may already have a lab relationship. Get two or three quotes for a single-marker GC-MS assay — pricing varies enough that it is worth asking rather than assuming.

Step 3

Test three different lots, not one.

This is the step people skip and it is the one that answers your real question. A single result tells you where you are today; three tell you how much your supply actually moves. If three lots come back within a narrow band, you have a good supplier and the problem is mostly solved. If they spread widely, you have found your variability and you need Step 4.

Step 4

Decide between specifying a range from your existing supplier, or moving to a standardised extract.

A range on milled herb is cheaper and keeps your whole-herb positioning. A standardised extract shifts consistency onto the supplier by design but costs more, changes your ingredient story, and moves every ceiling in this document — an extract is far more concentrated than herb, so the milligrams all have to be recalculated.

Step 5

Write it into the written spec and onto the purchase order, with COA-per-lot as a condition of acceptance.

A spec that lives in an email is not a spec. It belongs in a signed document your manufacturer builds to: botanical ID and method, plant part, form, thujone range, plus heavy metals, microbiology, pesticide residue — and mycotoxin screening on the black walnut.

Step 6

Assay the finished capsule once, at the start and after any formulation change.

Raw-material testing tells you what went in. A finished-product assay confirms the blending and encapsulation did not change it and that your arithmetic is right. Not needed every lot; very much needed once.

Step 7

Keep every COA and every retain sample, filed by lot.

If a regulator, a veterinarian or an attorney ever asks, the file is the entire difference between “we test our raw materials” and being able to show it. Retains also let you go back and test a specific lot after the fact, which is exactly what you would want in the one scenario where it matters.

Who does this

You specify it; your manufacturer executes it

You should not be running this yourself. If you have a contract manufacturer or co-packer, this is a conversation with their QA contact, and it is a routine one — they build to whatever spec they are handed. The reason it is not happening now is almost certainly that nobody has handed them a spec that mentions thujone.

Your formulator is the right person to set the target range, since they know what dose produced the results they saw. Your manufacturer is the right person to source against it and hold the paperwork.

Worth considering alongside this: NASC membership brings an audited quality framework that covers exactly this territory — raw material specs, per-lot COAs, adverse event reporting, label standards. It would give you a structure to work inside rather than assembling one from scratch, and the seal is meaningful to the kind of customer who reads ingredient panels.

Set expectations

What “right” looks like when you are done

You will not reach certainty, and you should not aim for it. What you are aiming for is a defensible position, which has three parts and no more:

  • You know what is in it — assayed thujone and eugenol per capsule, on file per lot.
  • You know why those numbers — tied to published limits where they exist, and to named, written reasoning where they do not.
  • Someone qualified has reviewed it — a veterinary toxicologist on the numbers, and for the label wording, counsel who works in this area.

Very few small pet supplement brands can show all three. Getting there is a matter of weeks and a few lab invoices, not a rebuild — and it converts your formulator’s years of experience from an assertion into something you can put in front of anyone who asks.

Part IV — Dogs and cats need different capsules

Cats are deficient in UDP-glucuronosyltransferase, the pathway most mammals use to clear phenolic compounds. Eugenol is a phenol.

The mechanistic detail makes it concrete. Eugenol at up to 600 mg/kg produced no hepatotoxicity in mice — but mice whose glutathione synthesis had been blocked developed liver damage at 400–600 mg/kg. Glutathione is the backstop. Cats are notoriously glutathione-limited, which is precisely why paracetamol is lethal to them at doses a dog shrugs off. The mouse depletion model is, functionally, a model of the cat.

There is no established feline ADI for eugenol — the veterinary literature does not provide one. That absence is the finding: you cannot look this up, so you build margin instead. Applying a further 10× to the human ADI on the basis of the two compounding deficits gives 0.25 mg/kg/day. That is a judgement call, flagged as one, but it is the direction any toxicologist would take.

Formulation fork

One capsule cannot be right for both species

The gap is about tenfold, and a disclaimer does not close it. Three legitimate options:

  • Formulate to the cat ceiling. One SKU at ~20 mg clove. Safe across both, but clove becomes close to a token inclusion for dogs.
  • Split the line. Dog and cat formulas. More SKUs, but each dosed properly — and it becomes a selling point rather than a compromise.
  • Drop clove from the cat version. Keep pumpkin seed and wormwood, which carry no equivalent phenolic problem. Cleanest if SKU count is the concern.
What decides it Your current clove milligrams. Near 20 mg and you are already fine — this becomes a labelling clarification. Near 150 mg, dog-appropriate, and cats on a full dose are the first thing to address, ahead of anything else in this document.
Rounding error

“1 capsule per 20 lb” quietly becomes “1 capsule” for every cat

A typical cat is 8–11 lb, so the label calls for roughly half a capsule. But a capsule is not a divisible unit in an owner’s hands. Faced with half, most people give the whole thing — it is easier, nothing gets wasted, and the label’s smallest named unit is one capsule.

The error is not random. It runs in one direction, always upward, in the species with the least margin. And it gets worse as the animal gets smaller, because the gap between the animal’s weight and the 20 lb reference widens.

A reason for cautious optimism while you wait. Clove is powerful stuff at the sensory level, and cats are famously unwilling to eat things that smell of it. Palatability tends to hold clove down in pet formulas whether or not anyone did the toxicology, simply because a bitter capsule gets refused and returned. If your cat customers have been dosing without complaint, that is weak evidence your clove load sits at the lower end. Weak — not conclusive, and no substitute for the number.

Do this week

The interim fix costs nothing and does not wait on lab work

Whatever the milligrams turn out to be, the rounding direction is worth correcting now, because it is free and it is the thing you control today.

  • Add one sentence to the product page and label: “Never give a whole capsule to a cat under 20 lb. If you are unsure, give less rather than more.” The instruction to round down is the entire fix.
  • Be careful how you tell owners to split it. Opening a capsule puts bitter, mucosally irritant powder in direct contact with the mouth — which an intact capsule avoids entirely, and which is a plausible reading of your one cat report. If owners must split, tell them to stir the powder thoroughly through a full portion of wet food, never neat and never sprinkled on top. Better still, stop asking them to split at all.
  • Email your existing cat customers. A short, calm note refining the cat directions reads as a brand that pays attention. It is also the record that you acted the moment you knew.
Then, at the next production run A lower-strength cat capsule dosed at one whole unit per cat. It resolves the rounding error, the clove ceiling and the go-slow start in a single change — and it turns the thing that is worrying you into the most defensible part of the range.

The reassuring part

Zero neurological events across ~80 animals

This is the number worth holding onto. Tremors, wobbliness, disorientation and seizure are the thujone signature — they are the endpoints the entire rodent literature is built around, and they are what a thujone problem would look like in a living animal. None have appeared.

It does not prove your thujone load is low. But of all the observations you could have, it is the one most consistent with a formula sitting where you hoped it was. Combined with the fact that both actual events were gastrointestinal and self-limiting — the expected profile for bitter, mucosally irritant botanicals rather than the profile of systemic toxicity — your record reads as a product behaving the way its ingredients predict.

Read honestly

Three limits on what 80 cases can tell you

  • The true rate is higher than 2.5%. Passive reporting catches the events people bother to write in about. Mild diarrhoea, a skipped meal, a dog that seemed off for a day — owners stop and say nothing. This is not a criticism of your record; it is how passive reporting works everywhere. Do not treat 2.5% as your number.
  • 80 cannot detect a rare event. At that sample size you are blind to anything rarer than roughly 1 in 30. Absence across 80 cases is not evidence of absence for the events you would most want to rule out.
  • Good outcomes were not measured either. “Nothing but good results” is the same kind of informal signal as the adverse reports — worth having, not worth citing as evidence to anyone outside the business.
Look again at this one

The cat case happened at the correct dose, not an overdose

One capsule split between two cats is, for two roughly 10 lb cats, approximately the intended label dose. So this was not a rounding-error event or an overdose — it is the one report you have of the protocol working as written, and both animals vomited.

Two readings, and the second is more likely:

  • Systemic: eugenol and the bitter principles reaching a dose the cats did not tolerate.
  • Local, and more probable: splitting means opening. Those cats met loose powder in the mouth rather than an intact capsule that would have carried it past the oral mucosa entirely. Clove is a known mucous-membrane irritant, wormwood is intensely bitter, and cats hypersalivate and vomit on bitter oral exposure more readily than most species.

The distinction matters because it points to a different fix. If it is local irritation, the problem is the route, not the dose — and no reduction in milligrams solves it while owners are still opening capsules.

Correction to earlier advice in Part IV I previously suggested telling owners to open the capsule and mix half the powder into food. Given this report, that guidance may describe the exact circumstances of your one cat incident. It has been revised: if splitting is unavoidable, the powder must be stirred right through a full portion of wet food — but the real answer is a cat capsule sized so nobody has to open anything.
The question that changes the reading

How many of the ~80 were cats?

This single number moves the interpretation more than anything else in the record. If 40 were cats, one GI event is unremarkable and your cat exposure is probably fine. If 5 were cats, then one event in five is a signal, and it is a signal in precisely the species where the toxicology already said margin was thinnest.

Worth reconstructing from your order history before anything else in this document.

Do this while recall is fresh

Turn the record into a record

Right now this history lives in your memory, which means it is real but unusable — you cannot show it to a veterinarian, a regulator, or an insurer. Writing it down changes that, and it costs an afternoon.

Reconstruct what you can for every case you remember, and log every case going forward:

  • Species and body weight
  • Dose actually given, and whether the capsule was opened — you now know that variable matters
  • Day of onset relative to starting
  • Signs observed, and how they resolved
  • Whether a veterinarian was consulted, and the outcome
  • Product lot number, if you can recover it

That last one is quietly the most valuable: lot numbers are what let you connect an event to a raw material batch once you start assaying. It is also the backbone of the adverse-event reporting NASC membership requires, so you would be building toward that rather than doing separate work.

Part VI — The rest of the formula

Keep

The AM/PM split does more than convenience

Thujone is a GABAA antagonist, and antagonist neurotoxicity is driven by peak plasma concentration rather than total daily exposure. Splitting a multi-capsule dose lowers Cmax directly. It has been promoted to a firm instruction in the Part I panel rather than the softer conditional it was.

No concern

Pumpkin seed is your free ingredient

Cucurbitin, the studied anthelmintic principle, is reported to have essentially no side effects, with only weak toxicity described in dogs and humans. There is no meaningful ceiling to work against. If you need to cut total actives to bring thujone or eugenol into range, pumpkin seed is what you increase to hold fill volume.

Fix the chemistry while you are in there: the compound is cucurbitin, a non-proteic amino acid (3-amino-pyrrolidine-3-carboxylic acid). Cucurbitacin B is a different molecule — a triterpenoid. Your copy currently calls cucurbitacin an amino acid, merging the two.

Supplier control

Black walnut needs a mycotoxin screen, not a dose ceiling

No established exposure limit to formulate against, and the canine safety literature concerns mouldy hulls and tremorgenic mycotoxins rather than the botanical itself. So it is a raw-material question: the COA should show mycotoxin screening per batch. It also carries the thinnest efficacy evidence of your four actives, worth knowing when you decide how much weight it gets.

Part VII — On wormwood itself

Settled, and it supports you.

The ASPCA toxicity listing that dominates search results answers a different question than yours: should an animal graze freely on this plant? It is poison-control triage for uncontrolled ingestion of unknown quantity. Garlic and chamomile sit on the same list. It is not an assessment of a weighed, assayed, cycled preparation.

The bodies that did assess wormwood for internal use reached a different conclusion — the EMA permits it in humans with a thujone cap and a duration cap. And there is genuine in vivo efficacy data: ethanolic extract produced 90.5% faecal egg count reduction in sheep at 2.0 g/kg, comparable to albendazole in that study, with separate work benchmarking it against praziquantel against Hymenolepis nana.

Be even-handed about the rest: several studies found strong in vitro activity that did not carry through in vivo at the doses tested. The honest position — “traditionally used, with supportive animal data, dosed to a recognised thujone limit, on a defined cycle” — is a strong one, stronger than most competitors can claim, and it requires overstating nothing.
