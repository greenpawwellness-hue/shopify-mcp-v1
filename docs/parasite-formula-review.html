Parasite Cleanse Dose Model

Working the exposure arithmetic behind 1 capsule per 20 lb — where the margin sits, where it thins, and the one specification that decides whether any of it holds.

Basis 1 cap / 20 lb / day Actives Wormwood · Black walnut · Clove · Pumpkin seed Status Model built, awaiting mg/capsule Revised 27 Aug 2026

The short answer

Your protocol is better designed than you probably realise. The exposure risk isn’t in the dose you wrote — it’s in the raw material you can’t see.

Three things in your dosing are quietly correct: the two-week duration, the AM/PM split, and per-weight scaling. Each maps onto something real in the toxicology. Your formulator built in the right controls.

But wormwood’s thujone content varies across roughly a 500-fold range depending on chemotype and origin. That means “200 mg of wormwood herb” is not a dose. Two batches with identical labels can differ by two orders of magnitude in the only compound that matters. Until you specify assayed thujone rather than herb weight, you don’t have a dose — you have a weight. That is the finding.

The second issue is species: the clove load that suits a dog is roughly ten times what a cat should get. One capsule cannot serve both well.

Part I — The model

Everything below runs off one conversion. Your label dose is 1 capsule per 20 lb, and 20 lb is 9.07 kg, so the per-kilogram exposure of any ingredient is simply its per-capsule mass divided by 9.07.

Exposure at label dose mg/kg/day  =  (mg per capsule) ÷ 9.07

Inverted — the ceiling you can formulate to max mg per capsule  =  (target mg/kg/day) × 9.07

Useful property: under linear per-weight dosing the mg/kg figure is constant across body weights. A 10 lb dog and an 80 lb dog get the same exposure per kilogram. So a single per-capsule ceiling governs the whole range — which is what makes the model tractable. (The exception is at the top of the weight range; see Part IV.)

That leaves two ceilings to set: one for thujone, one for eugenol.

Ceiling 1 — thujone, from wormwood

The EMA accepts 3.0 mg thujone/day for a human for up to two weeks. Across a 70 kg adult that is 0.043 mg/kg/day. Cross-checking against the rodent data: the benchmark dose lower limit for clonic seizures is 11 mg/kg bw/day, so the EMA figure carries a safety margin of roughly 250×. It is a conservative number, and in the absence of canine-specific data it is the most defensible target you have.

0.043 mg/kg/day × 9.07 kg  =  ≈ 0.39 mg thujone per capsule
Formulate to 0.35 mg and you hold a working margin.

Ceiling 2 — eugenol, from clove

The WHO acceptable daily intake for eugenol is 2.5 mg/kg bw/day, which for a dog gives 2.5 × 9.07 = ≈ 22.7 mg eugenol per capsule. Clove bud powder runs roughly 10–15% eugenol by weight, so that ceiling permits somewhere around 150–225 mg of clove powder per capsule. For dogs, clove is comfortable.

For cats it is a different calculation entirely, and Part III deals with it separately.

Part II — Why herb weight is not a dose

This is the headline finding, and it is the one worth taking to your formulator this week.

Thujone in dried wormwood herb is the product of two variables, each of which swings widely. Essential oil yield ranges from 0.2% to 1.5% of the dried herb. Thujone as a fraction of that oil ranges from near-nothing to 65% — β-thujone alone has been measured from 0.1% to 64.6% across samples. At least six chemotypes of European A. absinthium are recognised, and only some of them are thujone chemotypes at all; others are dominated by sabinyl acetate or epoxyocimene and are effectively thujone-free.

Multiply the two ranges and dried wormwood herb spans roughly 0.002% to 1.0% thujone — a 500-fold spread between the extremes.

Specification gap

Same label, same weight, 100× difference in the active

Both failure directions are real, and they fail differently. A low-thujone chemotype gives you a product that is safe and inert — customers see nothing, and your field results become unreproducible. A high-thujone chemotype puts you several-fold over the only regulatory reference point that supports the ingredient at all.

Neither is visible on a certificate that only says “Artemisia absinthium, aerial parts, 175 mg.” And batch-to-batch drift in a botanical is not a hypothetical — it is the default.

Action Add a thujone assay (GC-MS) to your incoming raw material spec, with a defined range — not just a maximum. State it on the COA per batch. Then dose to assayed thujone and let the herb weight float to hit it.

This is also the single best answer to anyone who challenges the ingredient: “we assay every batch and hold to the EMA reference” is a completely different conversation from “our formulator has used it for years.”

This may also explain your field data. If your formulator saw strong results in some periods and weaker ones in others across years of testing, uncontrolled chemotype variation is a more likely explanation than anything about the animals. Specifying thujone would make the formula reproducible as well as defensible — which is worth more to you than the compliance argument.

Part III — Dogs and cats need different capsules

Cats are missing the pathway most mammals use to clear phenolic compounds. They are deficient in UDP-glucuronosyltransferase, and eugenol is a phenol. That much is well established and widely cited.

The mechanistic detail is what makes this concrete. Eugenol at doses up to 600 mg/kg produced no hepatotoxicity in mice — but mice whose glutathione synthesis had been blocked developed liver damage at 400–600 mg/kg. Glutathione is the backstop. And cats are notoriously glutathione-limited; it is precisely why paracetamol is lethal to them at doses a dog shrugs off.

So the mouse glutathione-depletion model is, functionally, a model of the cat. A cat meets eugenol with both the primary clearance pathway impaired and the backup reserve thin.

There is no established feline ADI for eugenol. I looked; the veterinary literature does not provide one. That absence is itself the finding — you cannot look this number up, so you have to build margin instead. Applying a further 10× factor to the human ADI on the basis of the two compounding deficits gives 0.25 mg/kg/day, which is a judgement call and should be labelled as one, but it is the direction any toxicologist would go.

Formulation fork

One capsule cannot be right for both species

The gap is about tenfold, and it is not a gap a disclaimer closes. You have three options and they are all legitimate:

  • Formulate to the cat ceiling. One SKU, ~20 mg clove. Safe across both species, but clove becomes close to a token inclusion for dogs.
  • Split the line. A dog formula and a cat formula. More SKUs and more inventory, but each is dosed properly and it becomes a genuine selling point rather than a compromise.
  • Drop clove from the cat version. Keep pumpkin seed and wormwood, which do not carry the same phenolic problem. Cleanest option if the SKU count worries you.
What decides it Your current clove milligrams. If they already sit near 20 mg, you are fine as-is and this is a labelling clarification. If they sit near 150 mg, dog-appropriate, then cats on a full dose are the exposure to address first — ahead of anything in this document.
Practical

Most cats need a fraction of a capsule, and fractions don’t work

A typical cat is 8–11 lb, so the label calls for 0.4–0.55 of a capsule. In practice that means the owner opening a capsule and eyeballing half the powder, which realistically lands anywhere within ±50%. The species with the narrowest margin is the one getting the least precise dose.

A lower-strength cat capsule dosed at one whole unit per cat solves the accuracy problem and the clove problem in the same move.

Part IV — Scaling, duration, and the parts you got right

Keep

The AM/PM split is doing more work than you think

Thujone acts as a GABAA antagonist, and antagonist neurotoxicity is driven by peak plasma concentration rather than total daily exposure. Splitting a multi-capsule dose across morning and evening lowers Cmax directly. Whether or not it was chosen for that reason, it is pharmacologically the right instruction, and it should be a firm directive rather than the softer “if more than 1 capsule/day.”

Rewrite

“Best used in 2-week intervals” is ambiguous, and it’s load-bearing

Your entire safety case for wormwood rests on the EMA’s two-week duration cap. That cap means fourteen days of continuous use, then stop. But “used in 2-week intervals” can just as easily be read as “every two weeks, ongoing” — which is continuous use, and which dissolves the one control the formula most depends on.

A customer reading it the wrong way is not misusing your product. They are following your label.

Direction State the cycle explicitly and unambiguously: 14 days on, then a minimum of 14 days off. Give the maximum number of cycles per year. This costs you nothing, and it converts your strongest safety argument from implied to documented.
Cap it

Linear scaling over-doses large dogs

Metabolic clearance scales to roughly body weight0.75, not body weight1.0. Linear per-pound dosing therefore tracks metabolism correctly at your 20 lb reference point and drifts high above it — by a factor of (BW ÷ 9.07)0.25.

Direction Cap the daily dose — 4 capsules regardless of body weight is a defensible ceiling — or move to weight bands with a deliberately flattened top end. Small dogs are unaffected: linear dosing under-shoots below 20 lb, which is the safe direction.
No concern

Pumpkin seed is your free ingredient

Cucurbitin, the studied anthelmintic principle, is reported to have essentially no side effects, with only weak toxicity described in dogs and humans. There is no meaningful exposure ceiling to work against here. If you need to reduce total actives to bring thujone or eugenol into range, pumpkin seed is what you increase to hold the fill volume.

Fix the chemistry while you are in there: the compound is cucurbitin, a non-proteic amino acid (3-amino-pyrrolidine-3-carboxylic acid). Cucurbitacin B is a different molecule — a triterpenoid. Your copy currently calls cucurbitacin an amino acid, which merges the two.

Supplier control

Black walnut needs a mycotoxin screen, not a dose ceiling

Black walnut has no established exposure limit to formulate against, and the canine safety literature concerns mouldy hulls and tremorgenic mycotoxins rather than the botanical itself. So this is a raw-material question: your black walnut COA should show mycotoxin screening per batch. It also carries the thinnest efficacy evidence of your four actives, which is worth knowing when you decide how much weight it gets in the formula.

Part V — On wormwood itself

Carried over from the first pass, condensed, because it is settled and it supports you.

The ASPCA toxicity listing that dominates search results answers a different question than the one you are asking: should an animal graze freely on this plant? It is poison-control triage for uncontrolled ingestion of an unknown quantity. Garlic and chamomile sit on the same list. It is not an assessment of a weighed, assayed, cycled preparation, and applying it to one is a category error.

The bodies that did assess wormwood for internal use reached a different conclusion. The EMA permits it in humans with a thujone cap and a duration cap. And there is genuine in vivo efficacy data: ethanolic extract produced 90.5% faecal egg count reduction in sheep at 2.0 g/kg, comparable to albendazole in that study, with separate work benchmarking it against praziquantel against Hymenolepis nana.

Be even-handed about the rest: several studies found strong in vitro activity that did not carry through in vivo at the doses tested. The honest position is “traditionally used, with supportive animal data, dosed to a recognised thujone limit.” That is a strong position. It is stronger than what most of your competitors can say, and it does not require overstating anything.

Part VI — One thing to check on the copy

Setting the wider claims review aside, since dose is the priority — one item is worth thirty seconds because it is invisible from the page itself.

Verify

Your SEO title may not match your on-page copy

You use “cleanse,” and your on-page copy may well be consistent about it. But the title tag currently indexed for /products/parasite-cleanse reads “Dewormer for Dogs | Natural Parasite Cleanse Supplement.” In Shopify the SEO title is a separate field from your product title and H1, and SEO apps frequently write it for you.

It matters because a dewormer is a drug by definition, and the title tag is among the first things a reviewer looks at. If the field is clean, this finding disappears.

Where to look Shopify admin → Products → Parasite Cleanse → Search engine listing → Edit. Check the same field on your two blog posts while you are there.

Part VII — What closes this out

The model is built. Four numbers turn it from a framework into an answer about your actual product.

01

Milligrams per capsule — wormwood, black walnut, clove, pumpkin seed.

Divide by 9.07 and every exposure figure in this document resolves for your formula specifically.

02

Assayed thujone content of your current wormwood lot.

The one number that decides whether Part II is a live problem or already handled. If your supplier can’t provide it, that is itself the answer.

03

Milled herb or extract, for each active — and if extract, the ratio.

A 10:1 extract moves every ceiling in this document by an order of magnitude. The model holds; the numbers don’t.

04

Whether the cat dose is the same capsule at the same per-pound rate.

Determines which of the three options in Part III applies.

Scope note. This is a formulation and toxicology orientation, not veterinary or legal advice, and the feline eugenol factor in Part III is my derived judgement rather than an established standard — flagged as such because you should be able to see which numbers are borrowed from regulators and which are reasoned. Before a formulation change ships, the thujone spec and the cat dose are both worth an hour with a veterinary toxicologist. The model gives you the specific questions to bring.
